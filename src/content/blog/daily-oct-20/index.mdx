The headline numbers undersell the session. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 1.02% to 52,495.88 and the Russell 2000 gained about 1.65% to roughly 2,773, while the S&P 500 added 0.27% to 7,711.70 (7,712.57 intraday print) and the Nasdaq Composite finished flat, down 0.02% at 26,424.82 (with the Nasdaq 100 slipping 0.14% to 29,486.67). Health care shares hit record highs, led by Lilly, and defensive sectors outperformed while the VIX fell 6.13% to 17.31. The Nasdaq has now given up the bounce from Monday after an early drop tied to weakness in Asian technology stocks, where the Nikkei fell about 1.3% overnight. The month-to-date gap is striking: at midday the Nasdaq 100 was down about 2.9% for October while the Dow was up about 3%. The S&P 500 sits roughly 1.4% below its October 6 record and the Nasdaq about 4% below its own (our arithmetic). Breadth improving is constructive, but a rally led by health care and small caps while the AI complex stalls tells us investors are reducing their exposure to the trade that drove the year, not adding to it.

### The Earnings Reaction Function Is Now Consistent

Several results today followed a pattern we have seen all week. General Motors beat estimates and announced a $20 billion buyback, then cut its full-year outlook, and the stock fell nearly 8%. GE Aerospace beat and raised its forecast and still declined. Howmet fell more than 5%. 3M jumped more than 5% on its report. After Citigroup, Johnson & Johnson and TSMC earlier in the week, the lesson is steady: with third-quarter profit growth expected near 30%, trailing beats are priced in, and stocks respond to forward guidance and cash generation. After the close, Netflix beat on earnings ($0.87 per share against $0.82) and revenue ($13.44 billion against $12.87 billion) and fell about 5% in after-hours trading on soft fourth-quarter revenue guidance, which extends the pattern.

### Oil: The Expiry Squeeze and the Post-Midterm Premium

We flagged yesterday that WTI’s front-month contract was thin ahead of its expiry, and today showed why. The November contract rose as high as $110.48 and then closed down 1.8% at $101.79, while the more liquid December contract settled at $98.28, a gap of about $3.50 between adjacent months. A steep premium for prompt barrels typically signals physical tightness, which PVM Oil Associates attributed to falling Middle East exports. Brent for December settled near $109.09 (after touching $111.99).

Kpler counts at least 18 vessels targeted in the Strait of Hormuz over the past two weeks, and Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said two “non-compliant” tankers struck mines they had planted. The Guards also pointed to undersea cables beneath the strait in what analysts called a veiled warning, though they expect any disruption to be regional rather than global. The more important signal is about timing. RBC’s Helima Croft and Deutsche Bank analysts both argued that the oil risk premium may understate the chance of escalation after the November 3 midterms, since the administration has said it will not attack Iran before then. Traders were hoping for diplomatic progress today, and an executive order easing some sanctions and tariffs on Russian energy exports added supply headlines, but prices closed well off their highs. Our reading is unchanged: supply announcements move prices intraday while security events set the floor.

### The Rate-Sensitive Economy Is Cracking

Housing data was weak. September housing starts fell to an annual rate of 1.221 million against 1.305 million expected, and pending home sales dropped 7.3% on the month. The 10-year Treasury yield held near 5.11% at midday, down about three basis points, and the two-year was around 4.76%, so lower yields are helping the Russell 2000 even as the data confirm that high rates are restraining housing. On the supply side, the Treasury reported a fiscal 2026 deficit of $1.992 trillion, with outlays of $7.4 trillion against receipts of $5.4 trillion. A deficit of that size, financed at a 10-year yield above 5%, is a reason long-term yields have been slow to retreat even when growth worries rise.

Today reinforces the shift we have described this month. Investors are moving from paying for AI exposure to paying for cash flow, balance-sheet quality and defensiveness. Health care at record highs, the Dow outperforming and the Nasdaq stalling are consistent with that. We continue to favor companies that fund growth internally and have pricing power, we are cautious on leveraged AI infrastructure and on stocks that need beats to be rewarded, and we regard energy as a reasonable diversifier while Hormuz flows are impaired. Small caps are benefiting from lower yields, but weak housing data argue against treating that as a durable growth signal. We would turn more constructive if guidance across technology holds up and AI credit spreads tighten, and more defensive if oil holds above $105 or if guidance cuts like GM’s become common.

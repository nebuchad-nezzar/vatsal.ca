Stocks extended their losing streak to a fourth session on Thursday as oil prices climbed sharply higher and bond yields pushed further into multi-year territory. The S&P 500 fell 0.6% to 7,591.70, the Dow Jones Industrial Average declined 0.6% to 52,064.10, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.7% to 26,081.72. The Russell 2000 underperformed with a 1% decline. Pressure was broad-based, reflecting renewed inflation worries and a market still adjusting to elevated energy costs ahead of the Federal Reserve’s mid-September meeting.

Crude dominated the narrative once again. Brent briefly topped $107–$108 a barrel—levels not seen since May—while WTI settled around $102.30, up more than 6% on the day and bringing its weekly gain to roughly 11–12%. The surge followed the latest escalation in the U.S.-Iran conflict, including recent strikes involving tankers and continued severe constraints on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump’s comments indicating he is not currently seeking a deal with Iran reinforced the sense that the disruption could persist. Higher energy prices are feeding directly into inflation concerns, and today’s producer price index added fuel: the August PPI rose 0.4% month-over-month (in line with expectations) and 5.4% year-over-year (firmer than expected). Core PPI was slightly cooler, though upward revisions to prior months limited the relief. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to around 4.94–4.95%, its highest in years, as markets priced a higher probability of a rate increase next week—now approaching 70% in some estimates.

The Treasury’s expanded long-dated buyback, announced the prior day at up to $6 billion, took place against this backdrop. While the larger size signaled willingness to support liquidity in the 10- to 20-year sector, the market response remained muted. Yields continued higher, suggesting participants are looking for either larger operations or clearer evidence that the rise in long-end rates can be contained.

Interesting individual moves cut through the softer tape. Apple gained 3.6% as investors digested the launch of its new $1,999 foldable iPhone—the company’s first major product event under CEO John Ternus. In contrast, semiconductors faced pressure: Nvidia declined roughly 2.3% and Micron dropped nearly 4.7%, weighing on the broader technology complex and the PHLX Semiconductor Index. Energy names held up better than most groups on the oil strength. Materials led the declines among S&P 500 sectors (down about 1.5%), followed by information technology. Nine of the eleven major sectors finished lower.

After the close, Oracle reported a strong first-quarter result that reinforced the durability of cloud and AI infrastructure demand. Total revenue reached $19.3 billion, up 30% year-over-year, while total cloud revenue rose 62% to $11.6 billion. Cloud infrastructure (IaaS) was the standout, surging 121% to $7.4 billion, with the company noting the delivery of an additional 850 MW of datacenter capacity. Remaining performance obligations climbed to $664 billion, up $209 billion from a year earlier. GAAP EPS came in at $1.56 (up 55%) and non-GAAP EPS at $1.92 (up 30%). The print highlights continued strength in hyperscaler and enterprise enterprise spending on compute capacity even as the broader market contends with macro headwinds.

The session captured the market’s current tension clearly: durable demand for advanced semiconductors and data-center infrastructure on one side, and a macro environment complicated by elevated energy prices, firmer wholesale inflation, rising yields, and unresolved geopolitical risk on the other. Valuation sensitivity remains elevated, and tomorrow’s consumer price index will carry extra weight as the final major data point before the Fed meets.

The longer-term case for selective technology and AI-related exposure continues to rest on resilient fundamental demand for chips, data-center capacity, and related infrastructure. At the same time, concentration risk, elevated multiples, geopolitical uncertainty around energy supply, and the possibility of further monetary tightening still call for discipline and a focus on companies with clear pricing power and strong free-cash-flow generation.

Attention now turns to Friday’s CPI, residual earnings, and any further developments in the Middle East and the path of yields. Conditions remain fluid.

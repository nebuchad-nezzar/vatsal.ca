Yesterday the question was who pays for AI. Today the answer got more expensive. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 652.82 points, or 1.23%, to 52,215.16, the S&P 500 lost 0.53% to 7,622.99, and the Nasdaq Composite slipped 0.21% to 25,955.34 (with the Nasdaq 100 essentially flat, down 0.01% at 28,956.26). Technology steadied while chipmakers rebounded, and the selling moved to the rest of the market. The S&P 500 now sits about 2.5% below its October 6 record and the Nasdaq about 6% below its own. One number sums up the day: 6.5%, how much Brent crude has risen since Monday’s settle of $109.55, finishing near $116.66 ($116.72 spot settle).

### The Week in Five Acts

Monday was a relief bounce: the Nasdaq gained 1.1% after a rough prior week, while oil’s headline gain was a front-month expiry artifact. Tuesday was a rotation: the Dow jumped 1.0% and health care hit record highs while the Nasdaq sat still. Wednesday was the AI trade’s best moment, with SpaceX up about 7.8% and Super Micro up about 9% on hard evidence of demand. Thursday brought the reckoning, when Meta’s negative free cash flow and Jensen Huang’s warning about scarce capital took the Nasdaq down 2.6%, its worst day since June. Friday opened a second front: oil. The Nasdaq’s daily path of +1.1%, 0.0%, +1.1%, –2.6% and –0.2% shows why a reader looking only at the weekly total of roughly –0.7% would miss the story—three days of gains were erased in two. Across the major benchmarks, the week marked the Dow’s third straight weekly advance and a second consecutive weekly loss for both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq.

### No Place to Hide

Thursday’s defensive bid did not survive Friday. Procter & Gamble reported Thursday and jumped about 5%, its biggest one-day gain since March 2020 and a clear sign of capital seeking safety. By Friday, consumer staples, utilities and health care all fell alongside the broader tape. The 10-year Treasury yield rose about 4 basis points to roughly 5.00%, giving back about a quarter of Thursday’s haven rally, while December gold futures slid 1.34% to $4,006.00. Stocks, defensive sectors, gold and bonds all lost ground together as Caterpillar, Boeing and American Express led the Dow lower. Only energy worked, with the sector’s ETF hitting a record high. When traditional hedges fail on the same day, a portfolio needs a different kind of diversifier.

### Oil Takes the Lead

Brent crude rose more than 3% to settle near $116.66–$116.72, its highest close since April, while WTI traded firmly above $100. Reports indicated the United States and Israel are preparing for potential renewed strikes on Iran, while traders also weighed ongoing Houthi attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure. There is a political tension worth noting: on October 8 the administration stated the United States would not attack Iran before the November 3 midterms. While today’s headlines remain unconfirmed reports rather than final decisions, that timing gap is a primary reason the geopolitical risk premium keeps climbing into the weekend.

### The Loop That Worries Me

The reason oil matters now is that it connects directly to the AI story through a macro feedback loop. Higher crude lifts inflation expectations—reinforced today by the University of Michigan survey showing inflation fears edging higher. That leaves the Federal Reserve with less room to ease, anchoring 10-year yields near 5.0%. Those elevated yields raise borrowing costs for the AI infrastructure builders that rely on external funding—the exact vulnerability Meta and the neoclouds exposed on Thursday. Each link in the chain reinforces the next, which is how the same week can produce both an AI credit scare and an oil scare without any deterioration in underlying AI demand.

### The Economy Is Not the Problem

The macro data this week describes resilient real activity paired with subdued consumer mood. Initial jobless claims fell to 194,000 on Thursday, S&P Global’s flash manufacturing PMI rose to 56.0 against 55.5 expected, and services eased slightly to 57.9 from 58.8. The University of Michigan’s final sentiment index came in at 48.0, up from the preliminary 46.3 and essentially unchanged from September’s 48.1. American Express—which noted that affluent consumers continue spending briskly—captures the bifurcation: headline sentiment is weak, but the top of the income distribution is still driving aggregate demand. Strong economic activity combined with $116 oil gives the Fed little reason to turn dovish. Markets widely expect the FOMC to hold at its October 27–28 meeting, with rate futures pushing expectations for the next policy move into January 2027. Yesterday’s yield paradox—a falling risk-free rate alongside a rising price of credit risk—now looks like a brief haven bid that has already begun to reverse.

### The AI Complex: A Rebound Inside a Bad Week

The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index plunged 6.7% on Thursday and is down roughly 15% this month; even with Friday’s stabilization, it logged its longest weekly slide since November. Beneath the surface, dispersion widened sharply. Comfort Systems, a key contractor on data-center buildouts, fell more than 10% early on an unclear outlook, whereas cybersecurity leaders such as SailPoint, Cloudflare and Palo Alto Networks rose more than 5%, and cell-tower REITs rallied on reports that SpaceX is seeking thousands of terrestrial cell sites. Thursday’s internal breadth sharpens the distinction: the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 0.8% even as the cap-weighted index fell 1.2%, with 337 of 500 constituents finishing green. The first leg of this week's selloff was narrow and tech-specific; Friday’s weakness across industrials, financials and defensives shows the second leg is macro-driven.

### Earnings: A Beat and Raise Was Not Enough

American Express earned $4.68 per share against roughly $4.49 expected and raised its full-year earnings outlook, yet shares still fell about 3% after the company announced plans to reduce its global workforce by roughly 5% as it restructures operations around AI. That detail is a telling signal that AI is becoming an operating-cost and white-collar payroll story even while aggregate jobless claims sit near three-month lows. Elsewhere, SLB (Schlumberger) beat consensus with earnings of $0.64 per share against $0.62 expected, confirming capital deployment across the energy complex, while Robert Half surged nearly 9% premarket. In a market priced for nearly 30% Q3 earnings growth, even a clean beat-and-raise cannot insulate a stock when macro risk reprices.

### The Bull Case, Fairly Stated

A disciplined reading requires weighing the bull case. Third-quarter S&P 500 profit growth is tracking near 30%, jobless claims remain below 200,000, both manufacturing and services PMIs sit comfortably above 50, 337 S&P constituents rose even during Thursday’s tech rout, and the Dow just completed its third consecutive weekly gain. A 2.5% pullback in the S&P 500 and a 6% drawdown in the Nasdaq remain well within normal historical bounds for indices coming off record highs. The VIX closed the week at 19.45 (+2.21% on Friday after jumping 14.09% on Thursday), elevated from ~15 two weeks ago but far from panic territory. Bulls argue that the AI financing bottleneck is a solvable capital-markets friction rather than a demand problem—a point Jensen Huang made himself. My disagreement is about timing: building out credit and equity structures for $1 trillion in annual capex takes time, and the market is repricing the cost of capital right now.

### Three Ways This Could Resolve

This is my scenario framework rather than a point forecast:

* De-Escalation Path: Brent crude retreats below $105, inflation expectations cool, long-end yields ease back from 5%, and rate-sensitive equities alongside externally funded AI names lead a sharp recovery.
* The Grind (Base Case): Brent holds between $110 and $120 while U.S. growth data stays firm, the Fed maintains a hawkish hold, and elevated funding costs continue compressing multiples on leveraged AI borrowers while cash-rich compounders outperform.
* Escalation Path: Direct strikes resume or Strait of Hormuz transit is materially disrupted, sending crude sharply higher; in that regime, both Treasuries and traditional defensive equities are likely to fail as hedges simultaneously, just as they did on Friday.

I weight the Grind as the highest-probability path, which governs my positioning. Yesterday I outlined two triggers that would shift my posture more defensively: additional hyperscalers following Meta toward external financing, or Brent crude holding above $110. With Brent now settling above $112 for two consecutive sessions and closing Friday above $116, that second trigger has been met. I continue to favor companies that fund growth internally from free cash flow and hold visible order backlogs, remain cautious on AI borrowers reliant on capital markets, and view energy and defense as the two portfolio hedges that are actually working in this correlation regime. I am keeping duration short while oil and inflation expectations climb, and would turn more constructive if Brent falls back below $105, AI credit spreads tighten, or next week’s FOMC tone proves more measured than the macro data implies.

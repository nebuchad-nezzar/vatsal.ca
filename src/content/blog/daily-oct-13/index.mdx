The first day of third-quarter earnings produced a flat tape and a revealing reaction function. The S&P 500 slipped 0.02% to 7,805.45, the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.15% to 27,204.38, the Dow Jones Industrial Average lost 0.35% to 51,670.10 after being up more than 460 points at its high, and the Russell 2000 declined 0.30%. About 56% of U.S. issues fell. The S&P 500 sits just below last week’s record and is up roughly 14% for the year, against Nasdaq gains of about 17%. The significance lies in the bar the market has set: FactSet expects S&P 500 third-quarter profits to have grown close to 30%, so a beat is the baseline and a raise is the minimum. Johnson & Johnson fell about 5.7% despite beating revenue estimates and raising guidance. Citigroup reported its best third-quarter revenue in a decade and still fell roughly 5.5%, by Yahoo Finance’s account. When strong results do not lift prices, the market is telling us it has already paid for them.

**Banks: Strong Franchises, Narrower Margin for Credit Error**

The four large banks reporting today are best read as a split between capital markets and credit. Goldman Sachs earned $17.01 per share, far above consensus, with equities trading revenue up 72% to $6.44 billion. JPMorgan earned $5.57 against roughly $5.90 expected, its first miss in nearly three years, on weaker fixed-income trading, though it raised 2026 net interest income guidance to $106.2 billion. Wells Fargo narrowly beat on earnings but missed on revenue and raised its credit-loss provision to $1.03 billion. Citigroup beat, but credit-card profit fell on higher rewards costs and loan-loss provisioning. Management commentary across the group warned that rising rates and sticky inflation will weigh on consumers.

Two things stand out. Higher rates are helping the banks’ earnings power, which is why net interest income guidance is rising, while the same rates are the source of the credit risk now appearing in provisions. And the credit market is more cautious than equities: the high-yield spread has widened to about 315 basis points this week from roughly 260 in September, against a 2026 peak of 346, while the KBW bank index is down about 8.4% over three months as the S&P 500 is up more than 3%. Equities near a record and credit spreads widening is a divergence we would watch, because credit has historically been the earlier signal.

**Memory Stocks: Competition Narrative Replaces the Shortage Narrative**

The selloff in memory chips extended for a second day. South Korea’s Kospi fell 5.5% to 6,035.35 after dropping more than 7% intraday, with SK Hynix down about 10% and Samsung Electronics about 7.4%, both at their lowest levels since August. Micron fell roughly 4% to 5% in New York, and AMD about 3%. The trigger was China’s CXMT announcing progress on a denser memory architecture, with a more powerful chip planned by year-end. Analysts broadly see CXMT as still trailing the leaders, so we view the fundamental threat as longer-term. The more informative statement came from one strategist, who said investors are looking for excuses to take profits in AI-related stocks that have outperformed this year, especially memory.

That reading fits the pattern. Memory had the strongest narrative of any AI sub-sector, a supply shortage supporting pricing, and the market is now probing whether that pricing is durable. Micron’s strong quarter eleven days ago does not change the question, because the stocks were priced for it. The risk for investors is that a narrative shift, from shortage to competition, can compress multiples faster than earnings estimates fall, and the effects are visible in Korea, where the index is concentrated in a few memory names.

**Rates and the Fed: Yields Are Doing Some of the Work**

With the Treasury market reopened, the 10-year yield fell about 3 basis points to near 5.22% while the two-year edged up to about 4.80%, and the dollar fell against all seven major currencies. The catalyst was Federal Reserve commentary. New York Fed President John Williams said one more hike may be appropriate this year and none looks necessary in 2027, and Governor Christopher Waller said the surge in long-term yields is restraining housing and auto demand, though that is not the Fed’s intention. Taken together, the messaging frames the current rate level as close to a peak: a hike may still come, but the path beyond it is flat. That is why short yields held while long yields fell, and why the curve flattened. In effect, the bond market is tightening financial conditions on the Fed’s behalf, which reduces the need for further policy tightening.

This sets up tomorrow’s CPI as the test of that framing. Forecasts cluster around headline inflation of 3.6% to 3.7% and core near 2.4% to 2.5%. A core reading at 0.2% monthly would reinforce the one-more-hike baseline and support the long-duration assets that rallied today. A core reading of 0.3% or higher would make an October 28 move a live possibility and likely re-steepen the curve by pushing long yields back up. Gains in the S&P 500 are already close to record levels, so the asymmetry favors caution.

**Oil: The Spread Narrows for a Reason**

Brent settled down $0.96 at $105.69 while WTI rose $0.40 to $94.14, narrowing the premium of Brent over WTI to about $11.55 from roughly $12.90 yesterday. A narrowing spread points to tightness in U.S. product markets rather than relief in global crude. Goldman Sachs noted that Russian refinery outages are about 3 million barrels per day above year-ago levels, limiting how much additional diesel Russia can supply, while U.S. diesel inventories are down 14% from a year ago. President Trump defended the easing of Russian diesel restrictions on Monday despite bipartisan criticism. Our view remains that the arrangement can change sentiment faster than it can change supply.

**Geopolitics Reaches the Income Statement**

Johnson & Johnson’s results offer a concrete illustration. Its medical devices unit missed estimates for a second straight quarter, with the company’s chief financial officer citing the Middle East conflict, European austerity, and pricing in China. The stock also reflected a $5.5 billion talc litigation charge that cut GAAP earnings by 96% to $210 million. Last week it was Delta citing fuel costs. The pattern is that the Gulf conflict is now appearing in regional sales and cost lines, which suggests its effects are broadening beyond energy.

Today’s session reinforced three themes. Expectations are high enough that good results are not being rewarded. Credit markets and bank stocks are signaling more caution than the S&P 500 near its record. And the AI trade is differentiating between companies that capture the economics and those whose pricing power is now being questioned. We continue to favor companies with self-funded capital expenditure, diversified revenue and clear earnings visibility, and to be cautious on memory and component suppliers where narrative risk is elevated and on businesses with significant consumer-credit or fuel exposure. On duration, the Fed’s messaging supports a more constructive stance on the long end than a week ago, but we would wait for CPI before adding. Our view would shift toward adding risk if core CPI cools and credit spreads stabilize, and toward a more defensive stance if core inflation surprises higher or spreads move back toward the 2026 peak.

September CPI and the Beige Book arrive tomorrow morning and afternoon, with producer prices and TSMC’s results later this week. TSMC is the cleanest read on AI chip demand after the memory selloff. We are also watching whether high-yield spreads continue to widen, whether CXMT’s roadmap prompts guidance changes from memory suppliers, and whether any further Gulf escalation reaches Saudi export capacity. The Federal Reserve meets October 27 and 28.

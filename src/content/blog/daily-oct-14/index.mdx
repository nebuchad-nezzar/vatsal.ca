Wall Street had its worst session since July, and the way it happened matters more than the size. The S&P 500 fell about 1.1% to roughly 7,720 and the Nasdaq Composite lost 1.88% to 26,692.87, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped just 0.09% to 51,621.48. The Russell 2000 fell about 2%. The trigger was a hotter September inflation report, but the pattern of the day points elsewhere. Treasury yields spiked on the release and then reversed, and stocks never followed them back up. If rates were the whole story, equities would have recovered with the bond market. They didn’t, which tells us investors were repricing something more specific than the discount rate.

**Inflation Is Broadening**

Consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% from a year earlier, against a 3.6% forecast. Gasoline, up more than 9% on the month, accounted for over half of the increase, and that part was expected. The part that moved markets was core inflation: 0.3% on the month against 0.2% forecast, 2.6% on the year, with shelter up 0.4%. Capital Economics described the core surprise as broad-based rather than driven by one-off factors. Energy-led headline inflation can be looked through. A core pickup cannot, and it arrives one day after the New York Fed’s John Williams suggested one more hike this year and none next year. That message now looks optimistic.

**The Market Is Ahead of the Fed**

Futures put the probability of a quarter-point hike at the October 28 meeting near 35% by the close, after touching roughly 54% in the morning, up from 22% on Tuesday. They also imply better than a 90% chance of at least one hike by year-end and a 40% chance of two. The Fed’s own September projections top out at 4.00% to 4.25%, well below where futures now sit. Analysts are divided on timing, with some forecasting October and others December, and the midterm elections a week after the meeting raise the political bar for moving sooner. What is not in dispute is the direction. The question has shifted from whether the Fed hikes again to how many times.

**The Tell: Bonds Recovered, Stocks Didn’t**

The 10-year Treasury yield jumped as much as 11.8 basis points to 5.335% on the release, then retraced to close near 5.19%, slightly below Tuesday’s level. The two-year spiked about 15 basis points to 4.95% before settling near 4.81%. In other words, the bond market absorbed the data and steadied, with strategists noting the 10-year stayed well off its recent high. Equities absorbed it and kept falling. For the first time in weeks, bonds also did their job as a diversifier. The divergence is the session’s most important signal: the damage was concentrated in a specific part of the market, not spread across everything with duration.

**AI’s Financing Problem**

That part was debt-funded AI infrastructure. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 4.3%, extending a six-day slide to about 11.6%. Oracle dropped 8.8% to $130.55, and CoreWeave fell more than 15% as its bonds slid to distressed levels. Dell lost about 11% and HPE about 6% after Barclays downgraded both, arguing that valuations have run ahead of any visible AI inflection, while Marvell fell 8.7%. According to S&P Global Market Intelligence, the cost of insuring against default for some AI borrowers, including Oracle and CoreWeave, hit record highs.

Credit is where this story has been building. Yesterday we noted that high-yield spreads had widened to about 315 basis points from roughly 260 in September, and that credit has historically moved before equities. Today equities caught up. Even a strong demand signal failed to help: ASML raised its fourth-quarter outlook and said customers are discussing orders for 2029 and 2030, yet its shares fell nearly 4%. The market is separating demand for AI from the financial structure supporting it, and higher short-term rates raise the cost of that structure directly. Uncertainty about when the OpenAI and Anthropic public offerings might provide new funding adds to the concern.

**Banks: Beats Are Not Being Paid For**

The earnings pattern from earlier in the week continued. Bank of America fell 4.7% after revenue of about $30 billion missed estimates, trading was soft, and a $1.2 billion Treasury fine over financial-crime monitoring cut 17 cents from earnings per share. Morgan Stanley beat on both earnings and revenue and still slid 2.7%. With third-quarter profit growth expected near 30%, good news is the baseline and anything less is punished.

**Oil Keeps the Inflation Risk Alive**

Crude rose in late trading, with WTI up about 1.4% to roughly $95.4 and Brent up about 0.8% to roughly $106.5, on reports of an attack on Saudi Arabia’s state oil company headquarters and continued Houthi hostilities. With Brent holding above $105, gasoline is unlikely to retreat quickly, which leaves October’s inflation report exposed to another energy-driven reading.

**No Cushion in the Valuation**

The S&P 500 trades at about 19 times expected earnings, an earnings yield near 5.3%, which is roughly what the 10-year Treasury now pays (our arithmetic). Bank of America’s October fund manager survey found that 37% would consider moving from stocks to bonds if the 10-year reaches 6%. The median S&P 500 stock is up less than 5% this year, so the index rests on a narrow group of leaders whose financing is now being questioned. With so little equity risk premium, the market has limited room for a negative surprise.

The market’s worry has changed. It is no longer whether AI demand is real but who can afford to fund it at these rates, and it is no longer whether inflation has peaked but whether it is spreading. We would separate AI beneficiaries sharply by balance sheet: companies funding growth from cash flow deserve a premium, while those relying on debt and on revenue that begins years from now deserve scrutiny. We remain cautious on component suppliers with stretched valuations and on businesses exposed to consumer credit. After today’s retracement, intermediate Treasuries offer better risk-reward than a week ago, though we would wait for the Fed before adding meaningfully. We would turn more defensive if credit spreads approach their 2026 peak or the 10-year closes above 5.40%, and more constructive if core inflation cools and AI credit stabilizes.

**What We Are Watching**

Producer prices and TSMC’s results arrive this week, with TSMC the cleanest read on AI chip demand after a harsh stretch for the group. We are watching credit spreads and default-insurance costs for Oracle and CoreWeave, any new financing announcements from AI infrastructure companies, and Fed speakers for signals on October. The Fed meets October 27 and 28, followed by the December 9 decision, and the Gulf remains the principal risk to the inflation path.

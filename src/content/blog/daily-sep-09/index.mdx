Stocks extended their recent slide on Wednesday as oil prices climbed back above $100 a barrel and the Treasury’s latest buyback announcement failed to calm the bond market. The S&P 500 fell 0.5% to 7,636.36, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 0.8% to 52,380.66, and the Nasdaq Composite declined 0.6% to 26,253.34. It was the third consecutive down day for the major averages, with pressure broad-based outside of energy.

Crude was the dominant story. Brent settled above $101, its highest close in months and the first time back over the $100 mark since July. The move followed the latest round of U.S.-Iran exchanges, including reported strikes on tankers and continued constraints on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Higher energy costs are once again feeding inflation concerns at a sensitive moment—just days before key CPI and PPI data and the Federal Reserve’s mid-September meeting.

The Treasury Department added another layer of focus in the morning. It announced it would buy back up to $6 billion of 10- to 20-year nominal bonds in its next operation, tripling the previous $2 billion size and exceeding the “at least $4 billion” guidance given earlier.<Sidenote number="1">**Treasury Buyback Context:** Treasury is raising the per-operation ceiling for liquidity-support buybacks of 10–30-year nominal bonds to improve trading liquidity in older long-dated issues. Markets treat it as a policy signal that the administration will lean against elevated yields. However, the scale remains small against quarterly financing needs, meaning a print above $4B carries more signaling value than direct supply impact.</Sidenote> The program is intended to improve liquidity in longer-dated issues and signal that the administration is willing to lean against elevated long-end yields. Markets, however, appeared underwhelmed by the scale. The 10-year yield pushed higher, briefly reaching levels not seen since late 2023, before settling near multi-year highs. The reaction suggested some participants had hoped for a larger operation relative to the size of the overall market.

Sector performance reflected the oil and rates backdrop. Energy was the clear standout, finishing higher. Industrials and consumer discretionary led the declines. Technology was mixed: the broader Nasdaq finished lower, though semiconductors showed pockets of relative strength, with AMD among the better performers. Apple traded softer following its first major product event under new CEO John Ternus.

Taken together, the session captured a market still wrestling with the same tensions that have defined recent weeks: durable demand for AI infrastructure and semiconductors on one side, and a macro environment complicated by elevated energy prices, sticky inflation risks, rising yields, and an approaching policy decision on the other. The Treasury’s buyback step is a meaningful signal of intent on the long end, even if the immediate market response was muted.

The longer-term case for selective technology and AI-related exposure continues to rest on resilient fundamental demand for advanced chips, data-center capacity, and related infrastructure. At the same time, concentration risk, elevated valuations, geopolitical uncertainty around energy supply, and the possibility of further monetary tightening still call for discipline and a focus on companies with clear pricing power and strong free-cash-flow generation.

Attention now turns to the remaining inflation data this week, residual earnings, and any further developments in the Middle East and the path of yields. Conditions remain fluid.

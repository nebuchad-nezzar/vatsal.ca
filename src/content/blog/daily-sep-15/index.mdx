U.S. stocks slipped further on Tuesday as rising oil prices and a sharp move higher in bond yields kept pressure on risk assets ahead of the Federal Reserve’s decision. The S&P 500 fell 0.4% to 7,585.73, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 0.6% to 52,093.11, and the Nasdaq Composite declined 0.8% to 25,981.57. It was another soft session in what has become a challenging stretch for equities.

The dominant story in the bond market was the 10-year Treasury yield. It climbed as high as 5.04% — its highest level since 2007 — before settling near the 5% mark. The move reflects a combination of forces that have been building for weeks: elevated oil prices driven by ongoing Middle East disruptions, sticky inflation readings, heavy government and corporate debt supply, and rising expectations that the Federal Reserve will deliver a rate hike on Wednesday. Markets are now pricing that outcome at well over 90%.

When the 10-year sits at or above 5%, the practical consequences show up quickly in the real economy. Mortgage rates typically move into the mid-to-high 6% range or higher, which sharply reduces affordability for many households. At these levels, owning a home starts to feel less like a standard middle-class milestone and more like a luxury available mainly to higher-income buyers, cash purchasers, or those already on the property ladder. The pressure is already visible in rate-sensitive sectors and is likely to remain a headwind for housing and related consumer spending as long as long-term yields stay elevated.

Oil continued to support the higher-yield narrative. Brent traded near $108 and WTI remained firmly above $100, reflecting persistent constraints on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, Houthi advances affecting Red Sea routes, and the ongoing outage of a key Saudi pipeline. The combination of energy-driven inflation risk and a resilient labor market has left the Fed with limited room to stay on hold.

Technology and growth stocks felt the rate pressure most directly, while energy names held up better. Breadth remained soft, consistent with a market still adjusting to the higher cost of capital.

The session underscored the same tension that has defined recent weeks: resilient demand in parts of the AI and infrastructure complex on one side, and a macro environment defined by elevated energy prices, rising yields, and an approaching policy decision on the other. The 10-year above 5% is more than a technical milestone — it is a clear signal that the cost of money has reset higher, with real implications for housing, corporate borrowing, and equity valuations.

The longer-term case for selective technology and AI-related exposure continues to rest on durable fundamental demand for chips, data-center capacity, and related infrastructure. At the same time, concentration risk, elevated multiples, geopolitical uncertainty around energy supply, and the reality of higher long-term rates still call for discipline and a focus on companies with clear pricing power and strong free-cash-flow generation.

U.S. stocks finished modestly lower on Monday as pressure on artificial-intelligence names and another climb in oil prices weighed on sentiment, even as broader participation outside the AI complex helped limit the damage. The S&P 500 fell 0.5% to 7,619.98, the Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.3% to 52,421.20, and the Nasdaq Composite declined 0.6% to 26,186.41.

The Nasdaq had been down as much as 1.3% early in the session before recovering most of those losses. AI-related stocks came under notable pressure after several industry leaders publicly cautioned that a slowdown in development may be necessary for the safety of humanity. The comments sparked a wave of selling in high-profile names and raised fresh questions about the pace of infrastructure spending and elevated valuations.

Semiconductors were particularly hard hit, with the PHLX Semiconductor Index dropping sharply. In contrast, several software companies that had previously faced disruption fears staged a strong rebound—Intuit rose 5.5%, Autodesk climbed 7.8%, and Adobe advanced 5.3%—as investors rotated within the technology complex. Oil remained a central driver.

Brent crude settled near $105.68 after approaching $110 earlier in the day, while WTI also held firmly above $100. The move briefly pushed the 10-year Treasury yield to 5%, its first touch of that level since 2023, reinforcing the higher-for-longer rates narrative ahead of this week’s Federal Reserve meeting. Markets continue to assign a high probability to a rate increase on Wednesday.

Geopolitical developments kept energy markets on edge. Houthi forces consolidated control over additional strategic territory, seizing the Hanish Islands in the Red Sea and advancing toward key cities, further tightening their grip on the Bab al-Mandeb corridor. A major Saudi East-West pipeline—an important Hormuz bypass capable of moving large volumes to the Red Sea—will remain largely offline for weeks following last week’s attack.

Fresh reports of vessel strikes in the Strait of Hormuz and the cancellation of planned Gulf-state talks with Iran added to the sense that supply risks are not easing quickly. Transit volumes through Hormuz remain heavily constrained. Over the weekend, the 18th BRICS Summit concluded in New Delhi under India’s chairmanship.

Leaders adopted the New Delhi Declaration, which emphasized local-currency trade, digital public infrastructure, counter-terrorism cooperation, and institutional reform. Notably, for the first time since the regional escalation, Iran, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia endorsed a unified clause calling for operational restraint, the preservation of commercial maritime safety in vital waterways, and the prioritisation of diplomatic channels. While the declaration does not resolve the immediate shipping disruptions, the inclusion of language on maritime safety from parties directly involved in the current tensions was viewed as a constructive diplomatic signal amid an otherwise tense backdrop.

The session captured the market’s familiar tension in real time: selective resilience outside pure AI infrastructure names on one side, and the combined weight of elevated energy prices, rising yields, and unresolved geopolitical risk on the other. Breadth was better than the headline indexes suggested, with more stocks rising than falling within the S&P 500, but the concentration of selling in high-profile technology names still set the tone. The longer-term case for selective technology and AI-related exposure continues to rest on resilient fundamental demand for chips, data-center capacity, and related infrastructure.

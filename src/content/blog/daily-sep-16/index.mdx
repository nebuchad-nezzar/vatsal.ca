The Federal Reserve delivered its first interest-rate increase in three years on Wednesday, and markets finished lower as investors digested both the decision and a distinctly hawkish message from Chair Kevin Warsh. The S&P 500 closed down 0.4% at 7,551.81, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1.2% to 51,461.90, and the Nasdaq Composite ended essentially flat at 25,978.42 after surrendering earlier gains. In a unanimous vote, the Committee raised the federal funds target range by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00%.

The move was largely expected after recent data pointed to elevated inflation, low unemployment, and resilient consumer spending. Warsh was clear that inflation remains too high and has been elevated for too long. He described the economy as solid, with strong capital investment.

Updated FOMC projections pointed to one or two additional rate hikes by next year, consistent with upward revisions to inflation forecasts and downward revisions to the unemployment outlook. Markets are now pricing a meaningful chance of further tightening, including a roughly coin-flip probability for another move at the October meeting. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note rose above 5.01%, the highest in 19 years.

Beyond the inflation impulse from soaring energy prices, Warsh also acknowledged that Treasuries have been pressured by competing for capital with the surge in corporate debt issuance. Longer-term maturities rose less than the shorter end of the curve, producing a notable flattening as the unanimous decision and the Fed’s clear commitment to fighting inflation restored some credibility after earlier comments that had downplayed the urgency of higher rates. At these levels, the practical consequences remain significant: mortgage rates in the mid-to-high 6% range continue to pressure housing affordability, reinforcing the sense that homeownership is increasingly out of reach for many households and is beginning to feel more like a luxury than a standard middle-class expectation.

Geopolitics offered no relief. Saudi warplanes struck targets in Yemen while Houthi forces launched fresh drone and missile attacks on Saudi cities and infrastructure, including the Red Sea port of Yanbu and the Khamis Mushait airbase. The East-West pipeline, a critical alternative route for Saudi crude that bypasses the Strait of Hormuz, remains largely offline following last week’s attack.

Shipping volumes through Hormuz stayed at severely reduced levels. The conflict continues to expand in geographic scope, keeping a firm floor under energy prices even as crude pulled back modestly on the day. Diesel prices hit new record highs, underscoring ongoing tightness in refined products.

Within equities, the divergence was striking. Energy was the weakest major sector, down roughly 3%. Transportation stocks also lagged.

Technology, by contrast, showed relative resilience and finished slightly positive as a group. After several sessions of heavy selling triggered by industry warnings about the pace of AI development, selective buying returned. Dell, Intel, and Nvidia all closed higher.

Intel found additional support from reports of potential collaboration discussions with SK Hynix, though the company later clarified that no decisions had been made. Broader semiconductor and AI-infrastructure names stabilized as investors began to differentiate between companies with visible near-term demand and those more exposed to longer-duration valuation risk. The modest rebound suggested the recent selling had created some short-term opportunity, even as higher rates continue to weigh on the wider growth complex.

The dollar strengthened for a sixth consecutive session, with the dollar index moving back above 100, adding another layer of pressure on commodities and multinational earnings. The session left the market adjusting in real time to a higher cost of capital against a backdrop of sticky inflation, heavy debt supply, and unresolved geopolitical risk. The Fed has now formally shifted into tightening mode.

The path from here will depend on whether energy prices and the broader inflation data cooperate — and on how long the current level of long-term yields persists. Selective exposure to companies with durable free-cash-flow generation, pricing power, and exposure to structural demand in semiconductors and AI infrastructure remains preferred. Concentration risk, elevated valuations, and a higher cost of capital continue to warrant discipline.

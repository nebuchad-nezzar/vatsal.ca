One number frames the week: -11.2%. That is how far the Nasdaq Composite closed below its October 6 record today, officially placing the tech-heavy benchmark in correction territory on a closing basis (by my arithmetic). On Monday, the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.49% to 24,509.76 (with the Nasdaq 100 dropping 2.31% to 27,205.20), the S&P 500 slid 1.92% to 7,416.59, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average shed 739.38 points, or 1.41%, to 51,608.17. It marked a third consecutive daily decline for both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq—closing out last week’s late-week slide after Thursday’s governance shock and Friday’s 6.5% year-over-year October PPI print—and unlike Friday, when the Dow managed to finish green, today’s selling spared nothing. The Russell 2000 lost roughly 1.6% to 2,740, leaving the S&P 500 about 5.1% below its own all-time high. Intraday volatility spiked as the VIX surged as much as 25% above 24 before settling near 22.42, while the short-term volatility ETF VIXY climbed 9% by the closing bell.

### What Hit: A Chip Rule, Eight Days Before It Would Matter

Today’s immediate catalyst came from Washington, building directly on last week’s upheaval. The Commerce Department issued an interim final rule requiring export licenses to ship advanced AI semiconductors to data centers in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil—shutting down the transshipment loophole that allowed Chinese entities to access American compute through third-country server hubs. Taking effect upon publication in the Federal Register, it is the first tightening of the export regime since the personal conduct scandal and board investigation that forced Jensen Huang’s sudden resignation after 33 years at the helm of Nvidia last Thursday, November 12. According to CNBC, the rule was drafted directly by the Treasury Department and the National Security Council following internal administration criticism of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Adding fuel to the governance fire, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly called for a “dramatic shakeup” at Nvidia today and urged the board to look outside the company for its permanent chief executive.

Nvidia shares fell 4.12% to $156.14—their lowest close since July 1, 2025, and roughly 16% below their November 6 close of $186.64 (by my arithmetic). AMD, Micron, and Intel fell alongside it, dragging the semiconductor complex down more than 4% at midday. In strict financial terms, the new rule’s direct hit to Nvidia’s near-term numbers should be limited, because management’s $108 billion third-quarter revenue guidance already excluded China AI compute. The vulnerability is indirect: overseas cloud and neocloud providers whose utilization rates quietly rely on Chinese tenants. The market’s reaction tells you investors are no longer pricing a single regulatory filing; they are pricing an export and governance regime that is actively tightening while the world’s most important chipmaker operates under interim leadership.

### Tomorrow’s Examination

Tomorrow after the closing bell, Nvidia reports third-quarter results—its first earnings release without its founder in the CEO chair. Bloomberg consensus sits at $2.47 per share in earnings on $109.15 billion in revenue, stacked against the company’s own $108 billion guide. Longtime CFO and newly appointed Interim CEO Colette Kress will lead the call alongside newly named Chairman Harvey C. Jones, fielding questions that will range far beyond rack shipments into the external CEO search and Washington’s export posture. 

When the release hits the tape, I will read three items in strict order: fourth-quarter revenue and gross-margin guidance, any quantitative color on how the five-country licensing rule affects overseas sovereign and neocloud backlog, and the board’s tone on leadership succession. At $156 a share, the stock has already absorbed a severe governance and regulatory markdown, creating sharp two-way asymmetry: a clean beat-and-raise can trigger a violent short squeeze, whereas any hesitation on fourth-quarter guidance would land on a tape where sentiment is already deeply fragile.

### The Fed Chair Raises the Possibility of a Burst Bubble—and Four Outages Prove His Point

The most revealing macro event of the day came from Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh’s opening remarks at a symposium on artificial intelligence and the economy. Warsh disclosed that Fed staff are actively modeling both the labor-market displacement of AI and the macroeconomic fallout of a **burst AI bubble**—noting that while U.S. productivity growth is reasonably firm, it has not visibly broken out, and AI’s transformative economic payoff remains further in the future. While he added that the United States cannot maintain its superpower status without leading in AI and offered no explicit rate guidance, CNBC rightly framed the speech as a warning that AI could “disappoint” on near-term macro timelines. Having the Chair of the Federal Reserve put a burst-bubble scenario on the record on the exact afternoon the Nasdaq enters a correction is a remarkable moment—especially since the FOMC is still priced to hike rates again in December even as its Chair flags downside risks to the equity market’s primary growth engine.

If you want to understand *why* Chair Warsh is questioning whether hundreds of billions in AI capital expenditure are translating into real-economy resilience, look no further than the extraordinary sequence of operational breakdowns that began concluding late last week and spilled directly into this morning. Over a 96-hour window from Thursday, November 12 through Monday, November 16, four foundational pillars of American enterprise and consumer infrastructure suffered back-to-back systemic outages:

* **Microsoft 365 Global Outage (Nov 12–14):** Starting Thursday, a nearly 24-hour global outage crippled Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams, and Copilot across more than 70 countries, locking millions of enterprise workers out of email and calendars. Microsoft ruled out a cyberattack, attributing the collapse to a routine network capacity upgrade and a configuration change that overwhelmed core gateway servers.
* **FAA Nationwide Ground Stop (Friday, Nov 13):** Right in the middle of the Microsoft recovery, the Federal Aviation Administration issued a nationwide ground stop that halted U.S. flight departures for over four hours. The culprit was not a hacker: a contractor transitioning legacy copper wiring to fiber-optic cable at a remote facility triggered a router malfunction that flooded the air-traffic control network with thousands of connection attempts per second, destabilizing legacy radar and comms equipment.
* **Workday Production Disruption (Nov 13–14):** Late Friday into Saturday, enterprise cloud giant Workday locked thousands of corporations out of their human-resources, time-tracking, and payroll systems after an internal disaster-recovery test “unexpectedly connected to Production databases,” leaving live enterprise data in an inconsistent state.
* **Major U.S. Bank Outages (Monday, Nov 16):** Capping the four-day cascade this morning, customers at JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, U.S. Bank, and Zelle were hit with widespread online and mobile banking outages and delayed transaction postings—again traced not to a coordinated cyber event, but to internal software and processing bugs.

Officially, authorities and corporate status boards confirmed that these four failures were completely unrelated. **Analytically, however, they are deeply correlated.** They represent what I call the **Maintenance Debt Paradox**: over the past two years, Fortune 500 IT budgets and hyperscaler capex have been ruthlessly cannibalized to fund frontier GPU clusters and generative AI wrappers, starving core network engineering, legacy copper-to-fiber transitions, database isolation protocols, and core banking ledgers of operational redundancy. When Chair Warsh remarks that AI productivity has not yet broken out in the real economy, this is the physical evidence on the ground: corporate America is wiring $60+ billion at a time into frontier model training while its everyday digital plumbing cannot survive a routine router swap or disaster-recovery drill.

### Stocks and Bonds Moved Together, Reversing Last Week’s Playbook

In the Treasury market today, the 10-year yield fell roughly 3 basis points to 5.12% and the 2-year dropped about 5 basis points to 4.82%, with the 30-year trading near 5.47%. Fed funds futures now price roughly a 95% probability of a 25-basis-point rate hike in December and about a 5% chance of a 50-basis-point move—down from the 13% to 15% half-point odds seen on Friday after October CPI (+0.4% MoM) and PPI (+6.5% YoY, the highest annual wholesale reading since November 2022) rattled the bond market.

Notice how the cross-asset correlation flipped over the weekend. Last week, after Tuesday’s hot CPI and Friday’s PPI print, yields surged and equities fell in a classic **inflation regime** (rates up, stocks down). Today, equity weakness itself generated a safe-haven bid for Treasuries, shifting us back into a **haven regime** (yields down, stocks down). Last week’s rate shock has by no means vanished—the 10-year yield remains roughly 30 basis points higher over the past week and the 2-year is up about 37 basis points—but bond yields did not cause Monday’s selloff. Today was an AI-specific valuation, governance, and regulatory repricing, and institutional allocators used the Treasury market as a bunker. We will get the October FOMC minutes on Wednesday, bracketed by appearances from Fed Governor Christopher Waller on Tuesday and Friday.

### Oil: Relief That Equities Did Not Notice

Meanwhile, crude oil delivered a massive disinflationary gift that the equity tape completely ignored. Brent crude settled down 3.55% at $92.44 per barrel and WTI fell 3.21% to $85.72, as recovering tanker flows through the Strait of Hormuz reinforced global surplus projections. The International Energy Agency now projects the global oil market will return to surplus this quarter, and Goldman Sachs expects Brent to average $88 in 2027 before easing to $78 by year-end. 

By my arithmetic from October 30’s levels ($113.43 on the January Brent contract and $104.12 on WTI), crude oil has plunged roughly 18% in little more than two weeks. Yet the Nasdaq just entered a correction. That divergence connects the entire macro puzzle: in late October, the market’s problem was an oil-driven inflation shock; in mid-November, oil has stepped out of the way, and the problem is AI valuations, 5.1% long-end rates, chip export rules, and leadership turmoil. (BMI does caution that the recovery in OPEC+ export flows remains fragile until Washington and Tehran formalize a settlement.)

### A Currency Tail Worth Watching

One cross-asset fault line demands close attention this week: the Japanese yen traded near 159.6 per dollar today, fractions away from the 160.0 threshold that has repeatedly triggered direct Ministry of Finance currency intervention in Tokyo, while the U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) pushed above 103 to a two-month high. I flagged the yen carry trade earlier this autumn, and the combination of a Federal Reserve still hiking into December against a cautious Bank of Japan keeps rate differentials stretched to the breaking point. A sudden, forced unwind or intervention spike in the yen would trigger mechanical deleveraging across global risk assets—and a tech market already down 11.2% from its highs is the exact environment where a currency-driven margin call does the most damage.

### The Week as a Four-Day Stress Test—and the Anthropic Liquidity Drain

The remainder of this week compresses three separate macro and market-structure exams into four sessions:

* **Tuesday, Nov 17:** Nvidia Q3 earnings after the close; U.S. October Retail Sales; Fed Governor Waller speaks. Focus: Q4 guidance, five-country export rule impact, CEO succession tone, and real consumer spending resilience.
* **Wednesday, Nov 18:** October FOMC Minutes released; Anthropic prices its record IPO after the close. Focus: How many FOMC participants debated a 50-bps hike in October, and where Anthropic prices relative to its marketed range.
* **Thursday, Nov 19:** Anthropic begins trading on the Nasdaq under ticker **ANT**. Focus: Aftermarket institutional demand and whether funds liquidate existing mega-cap tech holdings to pay for their ANT allocations.
* **Friday, Nov 20:** Monthly Options Expiration (OpEx); Fed Governor Waller speaks. Focus: Dealer gamma unpinning and institutional positioning after a binary week.

Anthropic’s IPO is just as important for market mechanics as it is for AI fundamentals. Offering 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78 per share—alongside a $26 billion concurrent private placement from its own silicon and cloud suppliers—the deal is set to raise roughly $61 billion to $64 billion in public proceeds at a fully diluted valuation of up to $1.4 trillion, making it potentially the largest initial public offering in market history, according to Bloomberg. While Reuters reports the institutional book is heavily oversubscribed with retail allocations reaching up to 20%, I take no view on the standalone valuation here. The structural question for the Nasdaq is liquidity: absorbing $61 billion to $64 billion of fresh equity in a single week does not happen in a vacuum. As Reuters analysts warned earlier this month, portfolio managers frequently fund mega-IPO allocations by trimming their most liquid existing technology winners—meaning Anthropic’s cash call is hitting the market at the exact moment Nvidia’s transition, the Fed minutes, and a Nasdaq correction are testing the very same pool of capital.

### Positioning and What I Am Watching

Going into last Friday, my framework held that I would turn more defensive if the semiconductor selloff broadened into profitable leaders, and more constructive if Nvidia delivered intact guidance while the 10-year Treasury yield held below 5.0%. The bearish branch of that tree has already partially triggered: AMD, Micron, and Intel sold off alongside Nvidia today, the Nasdaq has crossed the -10% correction threshold, and the 10-year yield sits stubbornly above 5.10%. 

Consequently, I am more cautious today than I was on Friday—not because of bond yields or oil alone, but because the correlation across governance risk, tightening export controls, enterprise IT fragility, and a $64 billion IPO liquidity drain creates a dense, binary minefield this week. I am not adding net exposure into this sequence. Within equities, I continue to favor profitable, self-funded hardware and infrastructure toll-collectors and mission-critical network/cyber resilience over leveraged AI borrowers, single-executive key-man stories, or companies exposed to gray-market chip transshipment. In fixed income, I am keeping duration short: at 5.12%, the 10-year yield is a genuine competing asset for capital, but it is not yet a reliable portfolio hedge against a Federal Reserve that is still hiking rates.

I will turn more constructive if Colette Kress defends Nvidia’s fourth-quarter trajectory tomorrow night, if Wednesday’s Fed minutes show minimal appetite for a 50-basis-point hike, and if the Nasdaq absorbs Anthropic’s Thursday debut without breaking to a new closing low. Conversely, I will ratchet defense even tighter if Nvidia’s guidance falters, if the 10-year yield marches back toward its October peak near 5.36%, or if USD/JPY breaks through 160 and triggers a carry-trade unwind.

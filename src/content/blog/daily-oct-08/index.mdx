Markets spent the first week of October celebrating record highs on the strength of one narrative, that AI demand is compounding fast enough to outrun a 5%-plus risk-free rate. Today, one data point put a number on how fast. The Nasdaq Composite fell 1.25% to 27,193.34, about 1.5% below Tuesday’s record close and among its weakest sessions since July. The S&P 500 lost 0.47% to 7,765.36. The Dow, insulated by its lack of mega-cap AI exposure, rose 0.10% to 51,231.64, and the Russell 2000 was flat at 2,794.13. One detail makes the session more informative than the headline: Treasury yields fell on the day, with the 10-year easing roughly five basis points to about 5.23%. This was not a rates-driven selloff. It was an equity-specific repricing, which tells you where the market’s current vulnerability sits.

**Anatomy of a $20 Billion Discrepancy**

The Financial Times reported that OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue was approaching $50 billion at the end of September, against the roughly $70 billion that had circulated in the market in recent weeks. The gap has a mechanical explanation: investors anchored on an estimated $40 billion August figure, then layered on the 70% growth rate OpenAI cited, and the resulting number was never OpenAI’s own. A further wrinkle is that OpenAI reports net revenue while Anthropic’s figures include gross revenue from cloud providers, so comparisons between the two are not like-for-like. On a reported basis, Anthropic’s $65 billion run-rate now sits above OpenAI’s.

A sober reading is that nothing about OpenAI’s underlying business deteriorated today. The company still reports 77% run-rate growth in the third quarter and 107% growth in enterprise, and one strategist noted on CNBC that the two leading labs combined are at roughly $120 billion in run-rate revenue from near zero two years ago. What changed is the price of the expectation. Valuation frameworks built on $70 billion now have to be re-underwritten on $50 billion, and by one estimate the implied revenue multiple moves from roughly 20x to 28x, although the two figures are not directly comparable. In a market priced for perfection, a 28% downward revision to the sector’s anchor datapoint matters regardless of how healthy the growth rate is.

**Where the Damage Concentrated: The Leverage Layer**

The selloff was not uniform across AI exposure, and the pattern is the analytical core of the session. Oracle fell 5.5% and has now lost nearly 30% this year. It was joined by Intel (down about 6%), Broadcom (about 4.4%), AMD (nearly 4%), Nvidia (about 2.9% to $230.48), and CoreWeave. Oracle’s sensitivity is structural: OpenAI is among its largest customers, its contract backlog stood at $664 billion last quarter, and its quarterly capital expenditure has risen to $28.5 billion from $2.3 billion two years ago. A Wall Street Journal report today added that Oracle and other technology companies are preparing to tap debt markets to fund further chip purchases, and a separate report that an Nvidia-backed Australian data center developer failed to draw its hoped-for funding support reinforced the same question.

That question is the real subject of the day: who finances the buildout when the demand signal softens, and at what cost of capital? Companies funding capex from free cash flow can absorb a revenue-estimate reset. Companies funding it with long-dated debt against a customer’s growth trajectory cannot do so as comfortably, particularly with the 30-year yield at roughly 5.6%. We would expect credit spreads on AI infrastructure borrowers to become a more important sentiment gauge than equity multiples in the coming weeks.

**A Related Footnote: The Anthropic IPO Overhang**

The OpenAI disclosure lands against an IPO calendar that is becoming relevant to price discovery. OpenAI’s offering has been pushed to at least next year, while Anthropic is widely expected to list before Thanksgiving, with its S-1 expected imminently. A public filing will force a level of disclosure on revenue quality, customer concentration, and compute commitments that the private market has so far deferred, and today’s episode illustrates how sensitive the complex is to the quality of those numbers.

**Rates: Peak Anxiety, Then a Technical Relief**

The bond market delivered its own drama earlier in the week, and it is the necessary backdrop. On Wednesday the 10-year touched about 5.36% intraday, its highest since 2002, with the 30-year near 5.73%. Fed minutes showed most officials anticipate one more rate hike this year while signaling that an October move is not urgent, and prediction markets assign roughly 77% odds to another hike before December. The Treasury’s $39 billion 10-year auction then cleared at 5.3% with a bid-to-cover of 2.77, the strongest since 2016, and a record 97.5% allocation to non-dealer investors. That is a meaningful signal: at these yields, genuine end-buyers are stepping in, which is why yields eased today and why the long end has not yet shown disorderly behavior. Initial jobless claims of 197,000, below the 200,000 consensus, confirmed that layoffs remain low, which keeps the “one more hike” narrative alive and limits how far yields can fall.

**Oil and Geopolitics: A Presidential Put on the Calendar**

Crude spent the morning higher, with Brent pushing above $104 on reports, including one in The Atlantic, that the administration was developing options for renewed strikes on Iran, and with a tropical storm curtailing Gulf of Mexico production. Prices pared gains after President Trump stated that the U.S. will not attack Iran before the November 3 midterm elections, citing “productive discussions” with Tehran. The blockade of Iranian crude, however, remains in force, and the Treasury sanctioned 17 more vessels in Iran’s shadow fleet today, with officials saying Iran has only about 20 million barrels of crude left afloat outside the blockade.

The analytical takeaway is that the president has effectively placed a date on the geopolitical tail risk. That is supportive for energy-inflation expectations through early November, but it is a deferral rather than a resolution, and a cost-of-living backdrop in which roughly 60% of Americans disapprove of his handling of the war gives the administration a strong incentive to keep crude contained until the vote. Markets should treat the next 26 days as a window of managed risk and the period after it as a genuine unknown.

**Cross-Asset Tape**

The VIX rose 2.2% to 15.41, still low in absolute terms and consistent with an orderly pullback rather than a panic. Gold added 0.5% to roughly $4,160 even with a firm dollar near 17-month highs. Bitcoin slid about 2% to roughly $81,650, extending its pattern of behaving as a risk-on, liquidity-sensitive asset rather than a haven. Global equities were softer, with the MSCI All Country World Index down about 0.2% and the FTSE 100 and CAC 40 each off roughly 0.8% during the session.

Our read is that today was not a regime change, but it was a useful stress test, and the market’s response is instructive. A single revenue-definition correction, with no change to underlying growth, was enough to take 1.5% off the Nasdaq and 5%-plus off the most leveraged AI infrastructure name. That asymmetry, where the downside to estimates is punished more severely than the upside is rewarded, is characteristic of a crowded trade priced for flawless delivery.

We continue to see the AI capex cycle as real and the demand data, including Micron’s results last week and OpenAI’s own growth rates, as supportive. Our concern is narrower and about capital structure: the market is increasingly financing a demand curve with debt, at the highest long-term borrowing costs in more than two decades. Positioning should reflect that distinction. We would overweight AI beneficiaries with self-funded capex, diversified revenue, and net cash, and underweight those whose growth depends on a single customer’s trajectory or on continued access to cheap debt. Duration should remain disciplined given the Fed’s still-hawkish bias, and any rotation into the Dow-style defensives that held up today deserves to be sized as a hedge rather than a thesis.

The September CPI and the Fed’s Beige Book on October 14 are the next major macro tests, particularly for the “one more hike” pricing. Anthropic’s S-1 and any further disclosure from OpenAI will shape how the market values private-lab revenue and, by extension, the infrastructure names tied to them. Debt-market reception of Oracle and its peers’ funding plans will be an early read on whether credit investors share equity investors’ optimism. And the early start of bank earnings will provide a window into credit conditions at current yield levels. A sustained move in the 10-year back above 5.36% would likely challenge equity valuations regardless of how the AI narrative evolves.

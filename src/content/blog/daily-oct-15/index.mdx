Equities stabilized after Wednesday’s rout, but the interesting part of the day was what bonds did. September producer prices rose 1.3% on the month and 6.3% on the year, roughly double the 0.5% to 0.6% monthly gain economists expected and the largest monthly advance since March 2022. Yet the 10-year Treasury yield fell about nine basis points to roughly 5.09%, its lowest level since September 24, and the two-year slipped to about 4.77%. By the close the Dow Jones Industrial Average had gained 1.08% to 52,177.69 and the Russell 2000 had risen 1.38% to 2,767.27, while the S&P 500 added 0.33% to about 7,744 and the Nasdaq Composite finished up just 0.08% at 26,713.02. That flat Nasdaq figure hides the day’s largest intraday recovery: the index was down roughly 1.5% at its low before climbing back, and the S&P 500 erased an early loss of more than 0.6%.

**Bonds Voted on Growth, Not Inflation**

A hot PPI normally pushes yields higher. Today it did not, because investors rattled by the technology selloff moved into Treasuries, as CNN noted. In effect the bond market ranked growth and financial risk above inflation risk for a second straight session. We would not read this as a recession call: retail sales rose a better-than-expected 0.8%, jobless claims stayed low at 199,000, and regional manufacturing surveys were firm. But the 0.8% gain in retail sales was only 0.2% excluding gasoline, which suggests much of the nominal strength is price rather than volume. Bonds are being used as a hedge against equity weakness rather than a signal that the economy is weakening, which is a meaningful distinction for portfolio construction.

**The PPI-CPI Gap**

The detail beneath the headline matters for earnings. Core producer prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.7% on the month and 5.0% on the year, the fastest annual pace since January 2023. Services prices rose 0.8%. Producer prices are rising at 6.3% a year while consumer prices rose 3.7% in the September CPI. That wedge can only close in two ways: companies pass the costs on to customers, which means more inflation ahead, or they absorb them, which means margin pressure. Neither is comfortable for a market capitalized on expectations of roughly 30% third-quarter profit growth. Fourth-quarter guidance, not third-quarter results, is where this will show up first, and it is a reason we pay more attention to margins in this earnings season than headline beats.

**TSMC: A Flawless Print and a Falling Stock**

The cleanest data point on AI demand this week arrived overnight and the market sold it. TSMC reported a record profit for the sixth straight quarter, with revenue of $46.7 billion above the top of its guidance range, earnings of $4.60 per ADR against $4.42 expected, and high-performance computing now 68% of sales. It guided fourth-quarter revenue to $52.3 billion to $54.3 billion, about 58% higher than a year ago at the midpoint, reiterated $60 billion to $64 billion of 2026 capital spending, and its chairman said demand is “extremely robust” with the gap between supply and demand still “quite big.” The shares still fell about 2.4%. The explanation lies in the margin guidance: gross margin of 66.4% is expected to come under pressure as the 2-nanometer ramp and overseas fabs dilute profitability. When even the strongest AI print of the cycle is sold, the market is no longer trading the beat. It is trading the second derivative, margins and capital intensity, and that is a higher bar than the one the group has been clearing.

**Asia as the Pressure Valve**

The pressure showed up most violently in Asia, where trading followed Wednesday’s U.S. selloff. South Korea’s Kospi fell 7.57% to 5,554.22, its lowest close since April, with Samsung Electronics down roughly 9.5% and SK Hynix about 11%. Japan’s Nikkei 225 fell about 4.9% to roughly 64,400, led by SoftBank (down about 9%), Tokyo Electron (down about 8%) and Kioxia (down more than 12%), and Taiwan’s Taiex dropped about 3%. The Kospi is now down more than 16% in a month and more than a third below its June peak above 9,000 (our arithmetic), a decline of a size that goes beyond rotation. Asia’s AI complex is concentrated in memory and equipment, the segments we flagged this week as most exposed to competition and positioning risk, and it carries currency and funding sensitivities that U.S. markets do not. What is notable is that the U.S. session absorbed the damage and recovered, which suggests the contagion has so far been one-directional.

**Rotation Under the Surface**

Beneath the index levels, the U.S. market rotated sharply. Health care was the strongest sector, up roughly 2.5%, while technology and energy lagged, and AI-infrastructure names such as Vertiv (down about 6%) and Caterpillar (down about 3%) fell at midday. Financial stocks, which carry far more weight in the price-weighted Dow than in the S&P 500, were cited as a driver of the Dow’s outperformance, and the Russell 2000’s gain suggests investors were also willing to buy domestic cyclicals once yields eased. Gold rose about 1.6% at midday to roughly $4,150, so both traditional hedges worked on the same day, which is not guaranteed in this regime. The VIX stayed near 16.5 after jumping 10.5% on Wednesday, so volatility has not normalized.

**The Fed: October Odds Fell as Inflation Broadened**

The inflation data and the policy pricing moved in opposite directions. CME FedWatch put the chance of a quarter-point hike on October 28 at about 27% on Thursday, down from about 33% on Wednesday, even as inflation measures at both the consumer and producer level surprised higher. That is consistent with a market that sees the Fed reluctant to tighten into an equity and AI-credit wobble a week before the midterm elections, while still expecting a hike by December. Fed Chair Warsh has an interview scheduled ahead of the October 27 to 28 meeting, which is the next opportunity for guidance.

The market is separating two stories that have traveled together all year. Inflation is broadening, and it is feeding through producer costs into future margins. At the same time, the AI complex is being repriced on financing and margin quality rather than demand. Treasury yields falling does not repair the second problem, because lower government yields do not guarantee easier funding for heavily indebted AI borrowers, whose spreads matter more. We continue to favor companies that fund growth from cash flow, hold pricing power against rising input costs, and have clear earnings visibility, and we are cautious on leveraged AI infrastructure, memory and equipment suppliers, and businesses that cannot pass on costs. With the 10-year back near 5.1%, intermediate duration looks more attractive than a week ago, but we would stay measured ahead of the Fed meeting. We would become more defensive if credit spreads or the Kospi continue to deteriorate, and more constructive if margin guidance holds up through fourth-quarter earnings.

Tomorrow is monthly options expiration, which can amplify moves after a volatile week. We are tracking credit spreads and default-insurance costs for AI borrowers, whether Asia’s selloff stabilizes at the open, and margin commentary from companies reporting next week. Chair Warsh’s interview and the October 27 to 28 Fed meeting are the main policy events, and the Gulf remains the principal risk to the energy component of inflation.

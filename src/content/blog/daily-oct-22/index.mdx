All week I have argued that the AI debate is about financing, not demand. Today the biggest supplier in the industry said so himself, and the market sold on it. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6% to 26,008.86, its worst day since June, and the S&P 500 dropped 1.21% to 7,663.45, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average finished flat at 52,867.98 (up 0.02%) and the Russell 2000 was little changed near 2,782. That spread, a roughly 2.6-point gap between the Nasdaq and the Dow, is the signature of a pure technology unwind. The S&P 500 is now about 2% below its October 6 record and the Nasdaq about 5.8% below its own (my arithmetic). The Nasdaq 100 fell 2.88% to 28,962.86 as the VIX jumped 14.09% to 19.03, and Asia set the tone overnight, with Tokyo Electron and SK Hynix each down about 6%.

### The Trigger Chain

Four things hit in sequence. First, Meta reported after Wednesday’s close that free cash flow turned negative, a deficit of $2.12 billion in the quarter, and raised the floor of its 2026 capital spending range to $135 billion to $145 billion from $130 billion. A regulatory filing showed an additional $825 billion of future spending commitments tied to AI infrastructure, and the company announced a new nonvoting Class C share class, distributed as a one-for-one dividend that works like a two-for-one split and takes effect January 19, 2027. Its five-year credit default swaps reached record highs, and the stock closed down about 7%. Second, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang told CNBC that neocloud demand is extremely strong but that there is a “scarcity of capital,” with the demand for funding far exceeding supply. Wells Fargo traders said the comments reignited concern that credit markets are full from a risk-appetite standpoint. Third, Tesla lowered its 2026 capital spending plan while signaling higher spending in 2027 and 2028 on Optimus, Cybercab and chip manufacturing, and closed down 3.1% at $374.58. Fourth, Lam Research gave disappointing guidance, pulling chip equipment lower.

### Meta Changes My Framework, and I Should Say So

Yesterday I grouped Meta with companies funding AI from operating cash flow. The filing detail shows that grouping no longer holds. A company with negative free cash flow, a record cost of default insurance, $825 billion of forward commitments and a new share class designed to raise capital is moving from internal to external funding. Strip out the roughly $9.9 billion legal charge and Meta’s quarter was strong, with operating income of $22.9 billion and earnings of $7.40 per share against $13 billion and $3.83 on a GAAP basis, and fourth-quarter revenue guidance of $73 billion to $76 billion above the $73.7 billion expected. The market is not questioning the core business. It is pricing Meta as an infrastructure builder that needs capital, and the Class C structure reads as a currency for dilution that preserves Zuckerberg’s voting control. Of the three companies I compared yesterday, only Tesla, with positive free cash flow of $1.53 billion and about $45 billion of cash, still fits the self-funded category.

### The Casualties

The selling followed the funding profile. SpaceX fell about 9%, which by my arithmetic erases all of Wednesday’s post-earnings gain, and CoreWeave dropped about 10%. Micron lost 4.8%, Nvidia 3.6%, and the VanEck Semiconductor ETF 4.8%. Amazon fell roughly 4.6%, Alphabet about 3%, Oracle 2.8% and Microsoft 2.2%, and GE Vernova, whose turbines power data centers, fell almost 10% at midday. Only a day earlier SpaceX’s comments on AI demand had lifted those same names, which shows how quickly the narrative can turn when the question moves from demand to funding.

### Rates: Lower Yields, Not Lower Cost of Capital

The bond market produced the day’s biggest paradox. The 10-year Treasury yield fell about 15 to 19 basis points to roughly 4.95% to 4.97%, below 5% for the first time since September 22 or 23 and among the largest one-day drops in over two years. The two-year slid to a five-week low near 4.65% to 4.69% and the 30-year to about 5.3%. Bloomberg attributed the rally to haven demand and fading expectations for Fed hikes next year. Hilbert Group’s Russell Thompson described it as a rotation into Treasuries to capture yield, which is why yields fell counterintuitively.

Falling government yields did not help risky borrowers, because the move reflected fear, not easier financing. Meta’s default insurance hit a record on the same day yields dropped. For readers who think lower rates should rescue the AI complex, this is the key distinction: the risk-free rate fell while the price of risk rose. Companies that need capital are also competing with the Treasury for it, which implies they may need to offer investors more.

### Oil and the Geopolitical Tail

Brent December futures jumped 3.72% to settle at $112.87 after touching about $113 intraday, a five-month high, while WTI rose above $99. Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia added to supply fears, and reports that Israel and the United States could resume strikes on Iran lifted the geopolitical premium. Iranian officials promised widespread retaliation and pointed to Middle East data center sites and undersea internet cables as possible targets, which links the oil story directly to the AI infrastructure theme.

### Where Money Went: Defense and Cash Flow

Lockheed Martin gained about 7% after beating earnings and raising its 2026 guidance on strong missile and fighter-jet demand, extending the defense-backlog theme from earlier this week. Blackstone rose 6% to 8% on a profit beat, Spotify jumped about 9% on improved margins, and defensive names such as Procter & Gamble held the Dow flat. Capital left growth that needs funding and went to businesses with backlog, margins and cash.

### Q3 Earnings Scorecard: LMT, WDC, HON, SPOT

### [LMT] Lockheed Martin

Clean beat across the board, record $292B backlog (~4x book-to-bill), and a second consecutive full-year guidance raise alongside a 24th straight annual dividend increase.

* Revenue: $20.7B (+11% YoY; beat)
  * Missiles and Fire Control: +24% YoY sales
  * Aeronautics: +13% YoY sales (all four segments posted YoY growth)
* Earnings & Profitability: EPS of $7.97 (+15% YoY; beat by ~$0.45–$0.48 vs. $7.49–$7.52 est.); Net Earnings of $1.8B (+14% YoY); Segment Operating Profit of $2.23B (~10.8% operating margin)
* Cash Flow: Cash from Operations of $3.4B; Free Cash Flow of $3.0B
* Backlog & Awards: Record $292B backlog (+27% QoQ / +63% YoY); ~4.0x book-to-bill driven by the ~$52.5B multi-year PAC-3 MSE award
* FY2026 Guidance (Raised):
  * Sales: $80B–$82B
  * EPS: $30.35–$30.75
  * Segment Operating Profit: $8.575B–$8.675B
  * Free Cash Flow: $7.8B–$8.0B
* Executive Commentary: CEO Jim Taiclet highlighted $3.0B in quarterly free cash flow and the record $292B backlog supporting Lockheed Martin’s 24th consecutive annual dividend increase.

### [WDC] Western Digital

Massive Q1 FY2027 top- and bottom-line beat with 1,870 bps of operating margin expansion, a 17% dividend hike, and Q2 guidance well above Street consensus.

* Revenue: $4.38B (+55% YoY; beat vs. ~$4.13B–$4.14B consensus)
* Earnings: Non-GAAP Diluted EPS of $4.64 (+161% YoY; beat by ~$0.53–$0.56 vs. ~$4.08–$4.11 est.); GAAP EPS of $4.63 ($1.78B GAAP Net Income)
* Margin Expansion: Non-GAAP Gross Margin reached 58.1% (+1,420 bps YoY from 43.9%); Non-GAAP Operating Margin hit 49.1% (+1,870 bps YoY)
* Cash Flow & Capital Return: Operating Cash Flow of $1.60B; Free Cash Flow of $1.38B; quarterly cash dividend raised 17% to $0.175 per share
* Q2 FY2027 Guidance (Strong Raise):
  * Revenue: $5.0B–$5.2B (midpoint ~$5.1B vs. ~$4.57B prior consensus)
  * Non-GAAP EPS: $5.60–$6.00 (midpoint well above Street estimates)
* Executive Commentary: CEO Irving Tan cited robust customer demand and disciplined execution driving the 161% YoY EPS surge and 17% dividend increase, while CFO Kris Sennesael highlighted sustained exabyte growth and pricing power.

### [HON] Honeywell

Solid organic growth (+7%) and 370 bps of segment margin expansion drive an EPS beat and full-year guidance raise following the aerospace separation.

* Revenue: $4.99B (+1% reported / +7% organic; slight miss vs. ~$5.01B reported est.)
  * Building Automation: +8% organic
  * Process Automation & Technology: +8% organic
  * Industrial Automation: +4% organic (impacted by divestitures)
* Earnings & Margins: Adjusted EPS of $2.27 (+34% YoY; beat by $0.10 vs. $2.17 est.); Segment Profit of $1.06B (+23% YoY) at a 21.3% Segment Margin (+370 bps YoY)
* Cash Flow & Backlog: Free Cash Flow of $688M (+15% YoY); Backlog at ~$19B (+7% ex-divestitures; ~1.0x book-to-bill)
* FY2026 Guidance (Raised):
  * Sales: $20.0B–$20.1B with Organic Growth raised to 4%–5%
  * Adjusted EPS: Raised to $8.40–$8.45 (from $8.05–$8.35)
  * Free Cash Flow: ~$2.1B
* Executive Commentary: CEO Vimal Kapur noted that organic sales growth exceeded the three-year targets outlined at the June Investor Day across all three segments, reinforcing confidence in Honeywell’s 2029 pure-play automation targets.

### [SPOT] Spotify

Subscriber net additions top guidance alongside 136 bps of gross margin expansion and above-consensus Q4 operating income guidance.

* User Metrics: Premium Subscribers reached 306M (+9% YoY; +6M net adds vs. 305M guidance); Monthly Active Users (MAUs) at 788M (+11% YoY; +11M net adds)
* Revenue: €4.97B (+16% YoY / +14% constant currency)
  * Premium Revenue: €4.49B (+17% YoY / +15% cc)
  * Ad-Supported Revenue: €482M (+10% YoY)
* Profitability & Cash Flow: Gross Margin of 33.0% (+136 bps YoY); Operating Income of €672M (+15% YoY; 13.5% operating margin); Free Cash Flow of €735M with strong liquidity
* Q4 2026 Guidance:
  * Revenue: ~€5.2B
  * Operating Income: €820M (above Street consensus) on a 33.8% Gross Margin
  * Premium Subscribers: Expected to reach ~316M by year-end
* Executive Commentary: Co-CEO Alex Norström emphasized compounding engagement across music, podcasts, and audiobooks alongside moderating costs, while CFO Christian Luiga highlighted sustained free cash flow compounding into year-end.

I read today as a market repricing the cost of funding AI, not the demand for it. I still see demand evidence as strong, from TSMC, Super Micro, SpaceX and now Huang himself. The unresolved issue is who pays, and the answer will show up in credit before equities. I favor companies that fund their buildouts from cash flow and have visible backlog, I am cautious on AI borrowers that depend on external capital, including neoclouds and now hyperscalers with negative free cash flow, and I regard defense as a sensible diversifier given record backlogs. I would turn more constructive if credit spreads and default-insurance costs for AI names stabilize and capital markets reopen to the neoclouds. I would turn more defensive if more hyperscalers follow Meta toward external funding or if Brent holds above $110.

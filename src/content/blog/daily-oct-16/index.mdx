U.S. equities closed their worst week since June with another broad technology selloff. The S&P 500 fell 1.1% to 7,659.44, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.1% to 26,140.03, the Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.3% to 52,002.41, and the Russell 2000 lost 1.7%. For the week the S&P 500 fell about 1.95% and the Nasdaq about 4.5%, its first weekly loss in five weeks, while the Dow gained 0.7%. The S&P 500 now sits about 2% below its October 6 record close and the Nasdaq roughly 5% below its own. The damage was narrow: all but seven of the 75 technology stocks in the S&P 500 fell, yet only slightly more than half of the index’s members declined. Six of eleven sectors rose, led by energy (up about 2.5%) and utilities (up 1.4%). Reuters also noted that although the S&P 500 is up roughly 12% this year, its median stock is up only about 6% and about 185 constituents have lost ground, which is the backdrop for how much of the index rests on AI-linked leaders.

**A Week in Five Acts**

The sequence is worth setting out, because it explains today. Monday brought a memory-chip selloff on competition worries and a Gulf escalation that pushed oil higher. Tuesday’s bank earnings showed strong trading and weak credit, and Asia’s AI complex cracked. Wednesday’s September CPI, with core inflation at 0.3%, repriced the Fed and sent the Nasdaq down 1.9%. Thursday’s producer prices were the hottest in years, yet bonds rallied as investors sought safety from AI stocks, and equities recovered from deep intraday losses even though TSMC’s record results were sold. Today brought a second leg lower. Chipmakers fell for an eighth straight session, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down about 3.8%, the longest losing streak since 2008. The market did not drift lower. It was repriced in stages, and each rebound was sold.

**The Core Problem: Revenue Has to Outrun the Interest Bill**

Our reading is that the week was about financing rather than demand. Bloomberg Intelligence estimates that the largest technology companies are heading toward $1 trillion of annual capital spending, much of it debt-funded. Most hyperscalers carry high-grade ratings, but the market is now interrogating the weaker links. CoreWeave fell another 12% today, bringing its five-session loss to about 25%, and its bonds now yield above 15%, a level that typically signals distress. Broadcom fell 4.5%, Micron 5.6%, AMD 6%, Intel 5.8%, Meta 3.7% and Alphabet 2.5%. Analysts expect third-quarter earnings growth above 30%, so this is not a story of earnings collapsing. It is that the return on this spending has to arrive before the debt service does, and with the 10-year Treasury near 5.1% to 5.2% and rate hikes still on the table, the required return has risen. We flagged on Wednesday that credit tends to lead equities in this kind of repricing, and this week supports that. Reuters also reported today that some current and prospective investors in Anthropic are weighing a lower valuation for its coming public offering, which is the private-market analogue of what public markets are doing.

**Oil: The Second Leg**

Crude jumped about 3%, with West Texas Intermediate near $100.87, after U.S. lawmakers introduced a bill to block President Trump’s arrangement to release more Russian diesel into global markets. That reaction contains a useful piece of information. Earlier this week we argued the diesel deal could change sentiment faster than physical supply, and today’s move suggests the market had priced a meaningful relief premium that the bill threatens to remove. ING strategists described a significant risk premium for the Middle East and the Strait of Hormuz, and an IEA plan to accelerate emergency stock releases did not hold prices down. With producer energy prices already up 10.8% in September, oil above $100 feeds directly into the next inflation prints, and a U.S. Bank strategist noted that high oil prices weigh on equity multiples, which is why investors turned cautious into the weekend.

Treasury yields were little changed on the day, and the Fed is widely expected to hold at its upcoming meeting, with Barclays now forecasting quarter-point hikes in both December and January. The cross-asset picture was less supportive than on Thursday. Spot gold slipped about 0.2% to roughly $4,163, so neither bonds nor gold offered a clean hedge as they had a day earlier, and the beneficiaries were energy and defensive sectors rather than traditional safe havens.

For most of the year the market paid for AI exposure on demand evidence. It is now charging for balance-sheet quality. Our positioning therefore separates the AI complex by funding: companies that finance growth from cash flow and hold pricing power are being treated very differently from those relying on debt and on revenue that arrives later, and we expect that gap to persist. We remain cautious on leveraged AI infrastructure, memory and equipment suppliers, and businesses that cannot pass on rising input costs, and we view energy and utilities as sensible diversifiers while oil carries a risk premium. We would keep duration disciplined ahead of the Fed. Our view would turn more constructive if the chip complex ends its losing streak alongside tighter credit spreads, and more defensive if CoreWeave-style bond yields spread to higher-quality issuers or oil holds above $100.

The main items are the Fed’s October 27 to 28 meeting and Chair Warsh’s scheduled interview beforehand, the coming earnings from the largest technology companies, which analysts say must demonstrate that AI spending is generating adequate revenue and cash flow, and the progress of the congressional effort against the Russian diesel arrangement.

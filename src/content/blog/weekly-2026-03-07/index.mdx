March 7, 2026

Investors stood tall against a formidable wall of worry this past week. At the top of the list was the war in Iran that sent energy prices spiraling higher which in turn pushed up global interest rates. With the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed, Gulf officials warned oil prices could spike even more as additional production is likely to be forced shut due to the lack of available storage across the region.

Friday Brent and WTI crossed above the $90/barrel rising ~30% this week alone. EU natural gas prices jumped more than 60% while diesel prices surged 50% this week. It’s not just energy.

Key supply routes for aluminum, fertilizers, and a host of refined products typically pass through the Strait on a daily basis. The Trump Administration offered a series of announcements intended to assure markets these disruptions would be short-lived, including introducing a scheme around U.S. backed tanker insurance and potential military transports.

Ultimately, however, President Trump, Israel and the IRGC gave no indication that either side was looking for an off ramp to the current hostilities with Trump demanding nothing short of unconditional surrender heading into the weekend. Expectations around the ECB and the U.S.

S. Fed rate cuts got pushed out amid the Iran re-calibration with global central bankers identifying duration as the determining factor of whether this major geopolitical event will ultimately factor into future monetary policy decisions. Away from the Middle East developments risk appetite struggled to find supportive headlines, outside a few individual corporate highlights.

The U.S. February employment report was disturbingly weak.

Non-farm payrolls printed at -92K with the prior months revised lower. The unemployment rate ticked up while the participation rate collapsed. Economists were quick to point out the numbers need context given that strikes and weather probably impacted the data, but the weakness also fanned fears that the AI revolution is causing wider dislocation in the jobs market.

Stagflation re-entered the vernacular some used to describe the current economic path. Also, private credit concerns lingered, exacerbated by a series of headlines that resulted in several notable equity drawdowns across the space. Given all the impediments to risk sentiment the selling in debt and equity markets largely remained orderly.

The VIX spiked up above 28 early in the week and again on Friday. The dollar index tracked higher but remained capped below 100. The U.S. 10-year yield finished at 4.14%. For the week the S&P fell 2%, the NASDAQ declined 1.2% and the Dow fell 3%. European bourses struggled; the German DAX cratered 6.7% and the French CAC 40 lost 6.8%.

Asia Pacific stock markets were also under significant pressure with mainland China exchanges sliding 0.9% and Japan falling 5.5%. Your BSD Global Tech Hedge Fund retreated 1.0% this past week. In corporate news Target and Best Buy initially rose after earnings topped watered down expectations, but showed signs of success in managements’ turn-around efforts.

Crowdstrike saw a relief rally after results and guidance met expectations. Broadcom and Marvell reported very strong quarterly results, but more importantly managements’ were confident enough to provide forecasts for two years out driven growth expectations from AI/datacenter business. Nvidia CEO Huang presented at the Morgan Stanley conference again making the case that compute demand is currently insatiable, while calling OpenAI’s OpenClaw probably the single most important software release ever.

His shares briefly dipped on a report that the U.S. government was set to dramatically expand export restrictions for AI chips globally. Late in Friday’s session the AI stack came under pressure on a report talks between Oracle and OpenAI broke down on expanded leasing at the Stargate data center in Texas.

Private credit remained the canary, and probably what investors would have been most focused on if not for Iran. BlackRock and Blackstone disclosed that some of their funds were forced to implement gates due to redemption requests exceeding fund limits. Western Alliance was forced to take a charge on loans tied to the First Brands collapse last year, which initially shone a light on pooled risk in the private credit space.

Investors head into the new week watching the oil market after an extraordinary rally tied to the escalating conflict in the Middle East. WTI crude has just posted its biggest weekly gain since the contract’s launch in 1983, raising fresh concerns about inflation pressures and the potential impact on Federal Reserve policy if energy prices remain elevated. The U.S. economic calendar is highlighted by the February Consumer Price Index report on Wednesday. Economists expect headline and core inflation to come in at a 2.5% annual rate. The data will be closely scrutinized for signs that higher energy costs could complicate the Fed’s path toward interest-rate cuts later this year.

On Friday, the core PCE price index, the Fed’s favorite inflation gauge, arrives along with January income and spending figures. The earnings calendar continues to thin out, with just four S&P 500 companies reporting. Oracle reports on Tuesday, with investors watching demand trends for AI-related cloud services.

Later in the week, reports from Adobe, Dollar General, Ulta Beauty, Lennar, and DICK’S Sporting Goods will provide additional insights into enterprise spending, housing activity, and consumer demand. Other calendar events include the Game Developers Conference in San Francisco, where Nvidia, Apple, and Ubisoft are expected to highlight AI developments, and several investor day events from companies including Lucid Group and TransUnion. Elsewhere, Asia remains at the forefront of the supply chain disruption, with a specific focus on the closing of China’s National People’s Congress. The Eurozone is navigating a “stagflationary shock” where energy deficits punish the euro, though narrowing interest rate differentials against the USD provide some support.

October 11, 2026

The most important thing about this week is that the index record on Tuesday was the least informative event in it. The S&P 500 gained roughly 1.15% to close at 7,811, just under its record close of 7,818.93, and the Dow posted its best week since early August. But the Nasdaq-100, which set its own record on Tuesday at 31,224, gave nearly all of it back to finish up only 0.24%, and the Russell 2000 slipped 0.9%. The tell was underneath: the equal-weight S&P 500 rose 1.58% and ended a seven-week losing streak, while technology was the weakest sector in the index and nine of eleven sectors finished green, up from three the week before. Last edition I argued that leadership was still concentrated in mega-cap AI names rather than broadening. This week the market did what I said it had not yet done, but it got there through a repricing of the crowded trade and not through a clean broadening. Capital rotated inside equities, away from semiconductors and toward utilities, staples, health care and software.

The catalyst for the chip reversal is worth reading carefully, because it speaks directly to the monetization gap I have been flagging. On Thursday the Financial Times reported that OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue was approaching $50B at the end of September, far below the $70B figure that had been widely reported. The Nasdaq-100 fell about 1.4% on the day, the SOX dropped 3.4% and finished the week down roughly 4.3%, and Arm (-13.4%) and Intel (-12.3%) took the worst of it. The detail that matters is that the gap looks definitional: OpenAI’s figure is based on net revenue, while Anthropic’s includes gross revenue from cloud providers. A $50B run-rate is still roughly double where OpenAI ended 2025, so this was not a collapse in demand. It was a collapse in the market’s willingness to assume the most generous reading, and the positioning explains why the reaction was so violent. Goldman’s prime book had net exposure to the Magnificent Seven near 22% going into the week, the highest since early 2022. In Edition 27 I argued that spend was running well ahead of monetized revenue and that the cash-flow arithmetic was deteriorating faster than the narrative. One revenue headline was enough to put that argument back on the tape. Software told the other half of the story, with IGV up 3.9% against SMH down 4.3%, an eight-point split inside tech that a QQQ chart hides. The market is now separating who builds the infrastructure from who gets paid for it, and it is no longer willing to price them as one trade.

The most interesting sector move of the week was power. Utilities led at nearly 4%, and almost three points of that came in a single session on Tuesday after Google signed a 20-year nuclear supply agreement with Constellation Energy. Constellation and Vistra each rose roughly 15%, with Vistra also helped by a Department of Energy loan offer of up to $4.2 billion. This is the physical-constraint thesis arriving in price form. I wrote last time about Microsoft’s reported power bottleneck; this week the market paid up for contracted generation as a scarce input to AI, in some cases more than it would pay for another layer of silicon. It is a cleaner expression of the capex cycle than any chip multiple, because it values duration and cash flow, which is what the rate backdrop is rewarding. The same logic explains why staples, energy and health care also led, and why health care rebounded hard from the worst sector the week before, with Humana up about 11% on Medicare Advantage quality data.

The bond market remains the binding constraint, and it escalated. The 10-year Treasury yield reached its highest level since April 2002, 5.3645%, before buyers stepped in and it closed the week near 5.24%, with the 2-year at 4.80% and the 30-year at 5.60%. The NY Fed’s estimate of the 10-year term premium is now near 0.98%, the highest since 2014, which confirms the point I made last time: this is a market demanding compensation for supply and inflation risk, not one pricing a soft landing. The auctions were better than feared, with the 10-year clearing at 5.300% on a 2.77 bid-to-cover and the 30-year at 5.618%, but demand for duration is conditional. Wednesday’s minutes showed most officials expect one more hike by year-end after the September move to 3.75%-4.00%, ISM services prices paid printed 74.0, and the Michigan one-year inflation expectation rose to 4.7%. A hiking central bank and a term premium at a twelve-year high is a poor setup for anything that depends on distant cash flows, which is exactly the part of the market that sold off. The dollar held near 102, gold futures rose 1.4% to 4,221, and bitcoin slipped roughly 2.5% to about 82,000. In options, the VIX closed at 14.84 and the curve stayed in contango, but SKEW rose from about 145 to 149. Investors were paying more for tail protection even as at-the-money volatility fell. That is a market that is participating but keeping a hand on the exit.

Geopolitics returned to the price of energy at the worst possible time. Brent settled near $105 and WTI near $92. Brent had slipped under $100 early in the week after the IEA accelerated emergency stock releases, then jumped about 4% on Thursday on reports of Pentagon plans for US strikes on Iran that could come before the midterm elections, continued tanker attacks in Hormuz and a Gulf hurricane that shut in roughly 500,000 barrels a day. Last edition I noted that Saudi export resilience had kept oil more contained than the headlines suggested, and that this equilibrium was fragile. It has now been tested. Energy is still the year’s dominant trade, up roughly 48% YTD, but the more important point is the interaction with the rate story. A 5.2% 10-year plus $105 crude is a double inflation impulse, and the Fed has told us it is not finished. Politics is now a market variable too: the November midterms are shaping both the timing of any military escalation and the tone of economic policy, and headline volatility is likely to stay elevated until both the election and the late-October FOMC are behind us.

Two idiosyncratic moves late in the week are worth filing under disruption pricing. On Friday the major wireless carriers fell roughly 9% after SpaceX’s spectrum purchase, while tower owners rose, with American Tower up about 12%. The market read the implication instantly: a well-capitalized entrant with a different cost structure threatens networks that took decades to build, and the damage is uneven within the same industry. Deere fell nearly 10% after the FTC and USDA opened an inquiry into repair and dealer practices, a reminder that regulatory exposure to a lock-in profit stream is a form of business-model risk. The capital markets backdrop is still open, with Anthropic reportedly expected to launch its IPO later this fall at a valuation as high as $2 trillion, but this week showed that the audience for new issuance will reprice quickly on revenue-quality questions.

Putting it together: this was a week where the index headline flattered a market that was quietly changing its mind. The AI monetization debate I framed last edition produced its first real stress test, the term premium confirmed that the rate regime is structural, and the oil price added a second inflation variable. None of this argues that the AI cycle is over, and a $50B run-rate is still extraordinary growth. But the risk has moved from “is AI real” to “who is priced for the most generous version of the revenue story,” and this week answered that with a six-point drawdown in semis. Next week concentrates the catalysts. The big banks open earnings season on Tuesday, giving the first read on credit quality under higher rates, and Wednesday’s September CPI is the event that can undo this tape, because a hot print revives the case for another hike at the October 27-28 meeting. TSMC reports Thursday, the first hard chip-demand evidence since the selloff. My posture is unchanged: favor earnings visibility and balance-sheet strength, and stay cautious on crowded mega-cap exposure into the inflation print.
